Theory

This page reviews the theory behind the reliability methods implemented in this package: the reliability index, the first- and second-order reliability methods (FORM/SORM), the performance measure approach (PMA), the distribution transformations, and the sensitivity analysis that makes everything differentiable.

1. Limit states and failure probability

A limit state function g(x, p) describes the performance of a design x under random parameters p. The surface g = 0 separates the safe domain {g ≤ 0} from the failure domain {g > 0} (or the reverse, depending on convention). The probability of failure is the probability mass of the failure domain,

\[P_f = \mathrm{P}[g(x, P) > 0] = \int_{g(x, p) > 0} f_P(p) \, \mathrm{d}p ,\]

where f_P is the joint density of the parameters. Directly evaluating this integral is difficult: it is high-dimensional, the failure region is rarely known explicitly (each evaluation of g may be a finite-element solve), and the integrand is concentrated in a tiny corner of the space. Reliability methods replace the integral with a geometric problem in a well-chosen space.

2. Standard normal space

All reliability computations in this package happen in the standard normal space u ~ N(0, I), where the density is rotationally symmetric and decays as exp(−‖u‖²/2) — most of the probability mass lives near the origin, and the distance from the origin has a direct probabilistic meaning. The mapping from the original parameters is handled by the distribution transformations:

  • Gaussian parameters: the exact affine map p = μ + L u with Σ = L Lᵀ (Cholesky).
  • Any univariate distribution: the Rosenblatt transform p = F_X^{-1}(Φ(u)), u = Φ⁻¹(F_X(p)) — see the textbook table in Distribution transformations.
  • Correlated non-Gaussian parameters: the Nataf transform, which solves the equivalent-normal correlation ρ₀ so the back-transformed parameters have the target correlation.

3. The reliability index

The oldest index, the Cornell index, β = μ_g / σ_g, is not invariant under reparametrization. The modern definition, due to Hasofer and Lind, is geometric: the reliability index is the distance from the origin to the failure surface in standard normal space,

\[\beta = \min_{u} \;\; \|u\| \qquad \text{s.t.} \;\; g(x, T(u)) = 0 .\]

The minimizer u* is the most probable point (MPP) — the failure point with the largest probability density — and p* = T(u*) is the MPP in the original parameter space. Because the density is rotationally symmetric, the probability content of the half-space tangent to the failure surface at the MPP is exactly Φ(−β):

\[P_f \approx \Phi(-\beta) .\]

This is the first-order reliability method (FORM) estimate: exact for linear limit states, asymptotic in β otherwise.

4. FORM: RIA and PMA

Reliability index approach (RIA)

RIA solves the Hasofer-Lind problem directly: minimize ‖u‖² subject to g(x, T(u)) = 0. With the multiplier λ the KKT conditions are

\[2 u + \nabla_u g^\mathsf{T} \lambda = 0, \qquad g = 0,\]

and β = ‖u*‖. The problem supports several limit states simultaneously (g vector-valued): the MPP is then the closest point on the intersection of the limit-state surfaces.

The MPP is also the linearization point of the output distribution: a first-order Taylor expansion of f(x, p) around p* gives the Gaussian approximation used by RandomFunction,

\[\begin{aligned} \text{muf} &= f(x, p_*) + \nabla_p f(x, p_*) \, (\mu - p_*), \\ \text{covf} &= \nabla_p f(x, p_*) \, \Sigma \, \nabla_p f(x, p_*)^\mathsf{T}. \end{aligned}\]

Performance measure approach (PMA)

PMA inverts the problem: it minimizes the limit state over the target sphere,

\[g_* = \min_{u} \;\; g(x, T(u)) \qquad \text{s.t.} \;\; \|u\| = \beta_t ,\]

with the margin convention (positive values safe). For well-behaved limit states g_* ≥ 0 if and only if β ≥ β_t, so the reliability requirement P_f ≤ Φ(−β_t) becomes the ordinary constraint g_* ≥ 0. PMA tends to be numerically better behaved than RIA: the sphere constraint keeps the iterate away from the degenerate point u = 0, and it avoids the equality-constraint failures that plague RIA when β is large.

Sensitivities

The design sensitivities of β (RIA) or g_* (PMA) follow from the implicit function theorem: the MPP solves a KKT system c(x, u(x), λ(x)) = 0, so

\[\frac{\mathrm{d} (u, \lambda)}{\mathrm{d} x} = - \left[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial (u, \lambda)} \right]^{-1} \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} .\]

This package differentiates through the inner optimization problem with ImplicitDifferentiation.jl, which is why reliability_index, performance_measure and reliability_constraint are differentiable with respect to the design.

5. SORM: curvature correction

FORM replaces the failure surface with its tangent hyperplane. The second-order reliability method (SORM) keeps the quadratic term: in coordinates with u_n along the MPP direction, the surface is locally

\[u_n = \beta + \frac{1}{2} u_t^\mathsf{T} A u_t ,\]

where A = -(\mathrm{I} - \alpha\alpha^\mathsf{T}) \nabla_u^2 g \, (\mathrm{I} - \alpha\alpha^\mathsf{T}) / \|\nabla_u g\| and α = u*/β. A Laplace-type (large-β) argument gives Breitung's asymptotic formula

\[P_f \approx \Phi(-\beta) \prod_{i=1}^{n-1} (1 + \beta \kappa_i)^{-1/2}, \qquad \kappa_i = \text{eig}(A) ,\]

where the κ_i are the principal curvatures of the limit-state surface at the MPP (positive when the surface curves away from the origin). The correction is inactive for linear limit states (κ = 0) and undefined when 1 + βκ_i ≤ 0 for some i, in which case the package falls back to FORM with a warning. SORM indices are evaluation-only: differentiating through the curvature computation is not supported.

6. What the approximations ignore

  • First-order output statistics. The linearized mean and covariance are exact only for linear f in Gaussian parameters.
  • Local MPP. The Hasofer-Lind problem is nonconvex in general; the inner solver finds a local solution, warm-started from the previous MPP. The deterministic multistarts of RIA/PMA mitigate this.
  • Tail dependence. FORM/SORM model the rare-event tail through the geometry of the failure surface at a single point; they cannot capture tail dependence or multimodal failure regions, for which importance-sampling or subset-simulation methods are needed.
  • Numerical derivatives. The default limit-state Jacobians are central finite differences; supply a jacobian function for exact derivatives.
